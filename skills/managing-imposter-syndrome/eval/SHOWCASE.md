# Showcase: Managing Imposter Syndrome

> Demonstrates the value of the `managing-imposter-syndrome` skill pack by comparing
> skill-guided output vs. a vanilla LLM response to the same prompt.

## Test Prompt

> Use `managing-imposter-syndrome`. I was recently promoted to Director of Product at a 200-person fintech startup and now lead a team of 4 PMs. I'm the youngest director by 8 years and the only one without an MBA. I avoid speaking in exec leadership meetings because I assume my ideas are naive, and I'm compensating by overworking (60+ hour weeks reviewing every PRD my team writes). Create a Managing Imposter Syndrome Pack with a trigger and pattern map, cognitive reframes for my top 3 triggers, an evidence bank template pre-populated with prompts for my context, a 4-week exposure ladder (starting with low-risk experiments like asking one question per exec meeting), and a script for talking to my VP about imposter feelings. Output: Managing Imposter Syndrome Pack.

## Key Differences

1. **Diagnostic depth in trigger mapping.** The skill output labels specific cognitive distortions (mind-reading, catastrophizing, all-or-nothing thinking, discounting positives) for each trigger and classifies each as a "confidence gap" or "competence gap." This diagnostic precision helps the user understand not just what they feel but why the feeling is distorted. The baseline identifies triggers and patterns effectively but does not name the underlying cognitive mechanisms.

2. **Multiple VP scripts for graduated vulnerability.** The skill version provides four distinct scripts: a VP calibration conversation (Week 3), a VP vulnerability conversation (Week 4), a peer director reality-check, and a delegation framing script for the team. Each script includes rationale for why it works and a contingency plan if it goes poorly. The baseline provides one VP script (well-written) but does not stage the vulnerability across multiple conversations.

3. **Evidence bank with "fear vs. evidence" table.** The skill output structures the evidence bank as a four-column table (Claim/Fear, Evidence Supporting, Evidence Against, Next Evidence to Gather), which directly confronts each imposter belief with counter-evidence. The baseline's evidence bank uses category-based tables (accomplishments, feedback, problems solved) that collect positive evidence but do not explicitly map it against specific fears.

4. **Maintenance and relapse protocol.** The skill version includes daily (2-minute) and weekly (15-minute) maintenance routines, explicit relapse signals ("you catch yourself line-editing a PM's first draft again"), and a 6-step relapse response protocol. The baseline covers ongoing maintenance with a frequency table (weekly, monthly, quarterly, annual) but without the specificity of relapse detection and response.

5. **Explicit safety boundary and risk analysis.** The skill output opens with a safety boundary ("no crisis or clinical signals identified; this plan complements but does not replace professional support") and closes with 6 risks (overwork relapse, VP conversation backfire, unsafe environment, delegation quality issues, plan too ambitious, clinical anxiety) with mitigations. The baseline includes a closing disclaimer but no structured risk analysis.

## Verdict

Both outputs demonstrate strong understanding of imposter syndrome management and provide genuinely helpful, personalized plans. The skill-guided version differentiates itself through diagnostic precision (cognitive distortion labeling, gap-type analysis), graduated support scripts, a fear-vs-evidence table structure, and a structured relapse protocol. The baseline is more concise and includes useful additions like a metrics tracking table, but the skill version's depth makes it better suited for sustained self-management over months.
